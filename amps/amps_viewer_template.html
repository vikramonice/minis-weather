    Temperature

    Red line: air temperature at 2 m with daily highs and lows. Blue dashes: wind chill, what exposed skin feels. Symbols every 6 h: clear, cloud or snow.

    Cloud and snow

    Grey: layers where the air is saturated over ice, so cloud is likely, by altitude (right scale). Orange ticks: model cloud base. Blue bars: snowfall as mm of water per 3 h (about 1 cm of fresh snow per mm).

    Wind

    Speed at 10 m in m/s (left) and knots (right). Arrows point where the wind blows to; letters give where it comes from. Dashed lines mark strong (10 m/s) and gale (15 m/s). AMPS publishes no gusts.

    Field weather notes

    The meteogram in words, written automatically by fixed rules from the AMPS numbers, not by a forecaster. Rules follow the International Antarctic Weather Forecasting Handbook (Turner & Pendlebury 2004), section on Maitri and Dakshin Gangotri. Amber: drifting snow (mean wind 20 kt or more, 14 kt on fresh snow), snow of 0.5 mm or more, thick low cloud (poor contrast and horizon), a storm pattern, or pressure falling fast. Red: blowing snow (35 kt or more) or whiteout risk (thick cloud with snow or drift). “Last run” compares the day with the previous AMPS run: a hazard seen in both runs is more trustworthy than one that just appeared.

    AMPS against the station reports

    Every update compares what this page showed with the 6-hourly reports from Maitri and Novolazarevskaya. The bias tells you how to read the meteogram: for example, if AMPS has run 2 °C too cold at night, expect nights about 2 °C warmer than shown. It builds up through the season.

    Pressure

    Sea-level pressure. Red shading marks a fall of 4 hPa or more in 12 h, often the first sign of a storm or rising wind.

    How this page works where the numbers come from
    1. 🌍

      1. The weather model: AMPS

      The Antarctic Mesoscale Prediction System (NCAR, for the US Antarctic Program) runs a weather model tuned for Antarctica on an 8 km grid, twice a day, and forecasts 5 days ahead. It is the model most Antarctic forecasters use.

      new runs at 00 and 12 UTC, online about 10 h later
    2. ⏱

      2. An automatic job collects it

      Every 30 minutes while a new run is due, a small program on GitHub checks for it and downloads only the numbers for the grid point nearest each of our five sites (a few hundred kB, not the gigabytes of the full model).

      every 30 min, 09:00–12:00 and 21:00–00:30 UTC; new runs appear within about half an hour
    3. 📈

      3. Charts and field weather notes

      For each site it draws the meteogram (temperature, cloud, snow, wind, pressure) and writes the field weather notes (the meteogram in words) by fixed rules from the Antarctic Weather Forecasting Handbook: drifting snow, whiteout risk, storm patterns, falling pressure. It also compares each day with the previous run: a hazard seen in both runs is more trustworthy. Every rule, its source and a worked example ↗

    4. 🔍

      4. A check against real weather

      It fetches the 6-hourly reports from Maitri and Novolazarevskaya stations (via Ogimet) and compares them with what AMPS forecast, so you can see how far to trust it, for example "AMPS runs about 3 °C too cold".

    5. 📱

      5. This page

      The last 8 runs (4 days) are published here, for any phone or laptop. For a second opinion, each site links to the same point on meteoblue (global models, coarser than AMPS). The model is a guide, not a forecast: what you see on site comes first.

    Data: Antarctic Mesoscale Prediction System (AMPS), NCAR, run for NSF. Values are taken from the nearest 8 km grid point. Model output only, not a forecast: gusts are stronger than the mean wind, and conditions on the spot come first.
